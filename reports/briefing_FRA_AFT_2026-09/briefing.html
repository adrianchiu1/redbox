Bottom line

  1. The interest bill is no longer a rate story, it is a stock story. The fixed-rate OAT stock still carries an average coupon of 2.14% against a marginal 10-year yield near 4.85% (Bund 3.50% plus today's 135 bp). Medium- and long-term redemptions run at €203bn in 2027, €246bn in 2028 and €281bn in 2029, against €198bn in 2025, on top of €140–180bn of net supply and a €200bn+ bill stock that rolls every year, all at the marginal rate. On the register alone that pushes general-government interest from 2.2% of GDP (2025) to 4.0% in 2030 and 5.5% in 2035 in the status quo (Philippe, hung Assembly), €17bn a year above the EC Debt Sustainability Monitor path in the strict file by 2030, mostly because the spread now starts at 135 bp.
  2. No scenario stabilises the debt ratio. Debt has risen from 86.3% of GDP in 2010 to 115.6% at end-2025, and every path keeps it rising to 2035. Redbox's statistical benchmark, which extrapolates each budget line on its own history, takes it to 130% by 2031 and 145% by 2035; the political cases give 131% under a Philippe majority, 130% under a Le Pen majority, 133% under a Le Pen hung Assembly, 144% in the status quo and 166% under Mélenchon. Consolidation of €10–25bn a year slows the rise; it does not stop it while the average coupon climbs towards the marginal rate.
  3. The arithmetic no longer ranks the candidates the way the market does. The RN's counter-budget of 6 October promises €140bn of net savings by 2032 and a deficit below 3% in 2030 ext. Delivered with slippage (€25bn a year from 2028, €105bn by 2032), a Le Pen majority still has the lowest deficit of the five political cases (3.8% in 2030, 3.5% in 2035) and saves €34bn a year of interest against the status quo by 2035, more than a Philippe majority (€18bn). The risk in a Le Pen majority is the 2027 repricing (190 bp) and whether the cuts are delivered, not the end-point. Mélenchon is the outlier: a spread of 250 bp rising to 300 bp and a primary balance €10bn a year worse add €21bn a year of interest by 2030 and €76bn by 2035.
  4. The wrong-way loop is real but slow. A 2027 spike costs little in 2027 itself: only new issuance reprices, so 2027 interest is €89–91bn across all six paths. Feeding the extra interest back through the deficit into issuance adds €4.9bn a year by 2035 even in the Mélenchon case. The loop is mostly a market-pricing loop (spread, ratings, index eligibility), not a fiscal-arithmetic loop, at least out to 2030. That is where to press the AFT: what stops the pricing loop, not the arithmetic.
  5. The stock is shortening at the margin, not yet in aggregate. Average residual maturity of the register peaked at 8.5 years in 2022 and is 8.3 years at end-2025 (the AFT's own figure: 8 years 142 days at 31 August 2026). Maturity at issue of fixed-rate paper has fallen from 12.6 years (2021) to 10.3 years (2026 to date), and 32% of the stock matures inside three years. The much-quoted €301bn for 2027 is €203bn of OATs plus €98bn of bills already issued; the term-debt wall is 2028–2029 (€246bn and €281bn, 8.4% of GDP in 2029, above any year on record).
  6. Rate sensitivity sits with holders who can leave. The OAT stock has a modified duration of 6.0 years and loses about €140bn of market value per 100 bp. Non-residents hold 57.5% of negotiable debt, the Eurosystem has run its French holdings down from €546bn to €488bn in six months and is still shrinking, and French life insurers (9.6%) are absorbing supply on record inflows that reverse when rates rise. The marginal buyer of 2027–2028 is not the buyer of 2015–2021.

1Where the stock stands

The Redbox register holds every OAT, BTAN, OATi, OAT€i and BTF the AFT has listed since January 1999: 1,603 securities, positions rolled back from the AFT's encours pages through its auction histories, with the office's own daily indexation coefficients for the 35 linkers (D-S10-007). At the 10 September 2026 snapshot the register carries €2,862bn nominal, €2,937bn once linkers are uplifted: bills €218bn, fixed-rate €2,404bn, linkers €241bn nominal (€315bn uplifted). The AFT's own total was €2,903.8bn at 31 August ext. The register runs 1–4% above the AFT on the fixed lines because the AFT publishes buybacks only in aggregate, so lines near maturity are overstated; Σ BTF reproduced the AFT total to the euro over 2009–2020 and linkers are within 3%.

Two features of the composition matter for what follows. First, the bill stock: BTF outstanding was €107bn at end-2019, €190bn at end-2025, and the AFT plans to hold it near €215–220bn through 2026 ext. A bill stock of that size is a rolling €200bn+ of one-year-or-less funding at the €STR-linked rate, which the engine below prices at the short end of the OAT curve. Second, linkers: 8% of the stock at nominal, 11% uplifted. Their uplift accrues as interest in the national accounts (ESA 2010 §4.46), so with inflation at 2% they contribute roughly €6bn a year to D.41 before any coupon; in 2022–2023 that was €5–8bn (Figure 8).

2The interest bill and the wrong-way loop

Where it has come from

General-government interest (COFOG 01.7, the package's GF01_7) fell from 3.8% of GDP in 1996 to 1.4% in 2020, on a falling average coupon rather than a falling stock: the stock rose the whole time. It was 2.0% in 2024 (€59bn) and about 2.2% in 2025 (€65bn, the AMECO-stitched value; the Eurostat outturn arrives in October). Net interest was already 1.8% of GDP in 2024 against a primary deficit of 3.9%, so the primary balance, not interest, made the 2024 deficit. That ordering is about to flip.

Why it rises whatever happens next

The register makes the mechanism concrete. The nominal-weighted average coupon on the fixed-rate stock bottomed at 1.54% at end-2022, was 1.97% at end-2025 and is 2.14% at the September snapshot (Figure 6). The lines maturing in 2027–2028 carry coupons of 0–1% (the 0% November 2029/2030/2031 and 0.5–1.25% lines of 2019–2022 issuance); they are replaced at 3.5–4.4%. The AFT's own new-issuance rate has climbed from 3.45% in February 2026 to 4.23% at the 3 September auction ext. Nothing a government does in 2027 changes the coupons on the €301bn that matures that year; policy only changes the rate on the replacement and the size of the addition.

The loop, priced

Redbox rolls the register forward (module ggfiscal.debt.simulate). Each year the engine matures what the register says matures, pays the coupons it says are owed, accrues uplift on the linkers at 2%, prices the bill stock at the short end, finances the general-government deficit (primary balance declared, interest endogenous) at the marginal OAT yield in the AFT's 2025 mix of tenors, and feeds the interest back into next year's deficit. The Bund curve is held flat through 2035 (3-month 2.5%, 10-year 3.50%); the issuance mix and a €215bn bill stock are the same in every scenario. What differs is the spread, the primary balance and growth. The primary balance is common to 2027 (the 2027 budget is voted before the election) and then moves by a declared number of euros a year, as an outturn, so growth acts on the ratios through the denominator. The reference is the status quo, Philippe with a hung Assembly: €2bn a year of consolidation, 0.5% real growth, the spread at 175 bp in 2027 easing to 150 bp. A sixth path, drawn dotted grey, takes the primary balance from Redbox's statistical benchmark (the line-by-line forecasts of benchmark_balance.csv, D-S11-002) with the status quo's spread and growth: it shows where the budget goes on its own momentum, without a political assumption. Every number is in the tables in section 5.

How big is the loop? Figure 9 splits each scenario's 2030 interest gap to the status quo into the spread (re-run the scenario with its spread but the status quo's primary balance and growth), the volume (its primary balance and growth with the status quo's spread), and the interaction, which is the loop proper: the extra issuance that the extra interest itself requires, priced at the wider spread. In the Mélenchon case the 2030 gap is €21bn, of which €18bn is spread, €2bn is volume and the interaction is under €1bn; by 2035 the gap is €76bn with €4.9bn of interaction. The statistical benchmark sits on the status quo to 2030 (within €1bn), because their primary balances barely differ until 2031; it parts company afterwards, when the benchmark ends and its primary deficit stays at about 2% of GDP (€2bn more interest than the status quo by 2035). The other three cases are €5–6bn cheaper than the status quo in 2030, the Le Pen majority mostly through volume (the €25bn a year), the Philippe majority mostly through the spread. The arithmetic loop is small on a ten-year view because interest is a flow of a few per cent of a stock that turns over slowly. The pricing loop is what moves quickly, and it has already moved: the spread's annual average has risen every year since 2021 (38 → 76 bp), it averaged 101 bp in September 2026 and is 135 bp today (Bloomberg, 11 October 2026), the widest since the 2011–2012 euro crisis, with three downgrades in 2025 (Fitch, S&P, DBRS) and negative outlooks at Moody's and Scope ext.

What to take into the room. The AFT will say, correctly, that the average cost of the stock adjusts slowly (3.16% on the OAT stock in 2025 by its own measure ext) and that an 8.4-year average maturity buys time. Both are true and both are why even the status quo gets to 4.0% of GDP by 2030: slow adjustment works against France now that the marginal rate is above the average. The question that matters is not the level of the spread but whether anything below the ECB caps it. Press on the three exits: the TPI conditions (France is in an EDP and off its path, so eligibility is not automatic), the index and ratings floor (a fourth agency below AA- changes some mandates), and what the AFT itself can do on volume (buybacks, tenor, the bill stock) when the spread is the problem rather than the curve.

To ask the AFT on the interest bill

  1. What average cost on the stock and what interest charge does the PLF 2027 assume for 2027 and 2028, and at what 10-year rate? Our register-based status quo gives €89bn and €105bn of general-government interest.
  2. How much of the 2027–2029 redemptions has already been bought back, line by line? The register carries €203bn, €246bn and €281bn of OATs maturing in those years gross of buybacks; the AFT's 2026 programme put 2026 medium/long-term redemptions at €175.8bn against the register's €154bn at unindexed nominal.
  3. Does the AFT model a spread feedback in its own debt-service projections, or hold the spread at the current forward?

3The shortening stock

The register's average residual maturity rose from 6.5 years (2009) to a peak of 8.5 years at end-2022 as the AFT sold 30- and 50-year paper into negative real yields; it is 8.3 years at end-2025 and the AFT reports 8 years 142 days at 31 August 2026, down from 8 years 180 days in June ext. The aggregate has barely moved. What has moved is the flow: the average maturity at issue of fixed-rate paper was 12.6 years in 2021, 11.2 in 2024, 10.4 in 2025 and 10.3 so far in 2026 (Figure 10, right). The 20–30 and 30+ buckets that took 15% of gross issuance in 2021 took 10% in 2025 (Figure 11).

Three readings of this, and the AFT will offer the first. (i) It is demand-led: the long end has lost the Dutch and German pension bid and the Japanese carry bid, so the AFT issues where the book is, which is 3–10 years. (ii) It is cost-led: with a curve that steepened 2024–2026 and a spread that is largest at the long end, the AFT is paying up least at 5–7 years and the maturity mix follows. (iii) It is a choice about roll-over risk that the AFT would rather not make explicit: every year of average maturity given up raises the share of the stock that reprices inside the next presidential term. On the register, 32% of the stock matures within three years, and term-debt redemptions climb from €198bn in 2025 to €281bn in 2029: 8.6% of the stock, back towards the 8.5–10% of 2009–2016 after a decade in which lengthening had brought it down to 7–8%, and 8.4% of GDP, above any year since the register begins. The scenarios hold the AFT's 2025 tenor mix and a €215bn bill stock in every case, so that they differ only in the spread, the primary balance and growth. In practice a debt office under a spread shock leans on bills and shorter tenors, which is cheaper in year one and is the mechanism by which a 2027 shock becomes a permanent feature of the average cost by 2032; on refinancing risk the Mélenchon numbers are, if anything, flattering.

The bill stock deserves its own line. BTF outstanding was 5.8% of the stock in 2019, 6.9% at end-2025, and the AFT's plan holds it near €215–220bn through 2026. At 2.5–2.7% that costs €5–6bn a year and is the one part of the stock that reprices to the ECB within a year: a 100 bp policy move is €2bn of annual interest with no lag, and the same figure is what a 40 bp widening of the BTF spread to €STR would cost in a stress.

To ask the AFT on maturity

  1. Is the fall in maturity at issue a demand observation or a policy? What would bring the 20–30 year bucket back to 15% of issuance, and is there an average-maturity floor the AFT would defend?
  2. The 2027–2029 redemption wall: how much of it does the AFT intend to pre-fund and buy back in 2026–2027, and does the PLF 2027 programme anticipate more or less than the 2026 €310bn?
  3. Under a spread shock, is the bill stock the shock absorber (as in 2008–2009, when it doubled to €214bn), or is there a ceiling?

Three holder groups, three different sensitivities

The Eurosystem is the one holder whose behaviour is known: it is leaving. French holdings under PSPP and PEPP fell from €546bn at end-2025 to €488bn at end-June 2026, about €10bn a month, with no reinvestment since PEPP ended in January 2025 ext. That is €110–120bn a year of extra net supply the market must absorb on top of the deficit, before any 2027 shock. The TPI is the counterweight, and its conditions (compliance with the EU fiscal framework, no severe imbalances, sustainability) are exactly the ones France is furthest from. Whether the Governing Council would treat a post-election OAT sell-off as "unwarranted, disorderly dynamics" is the single most important unknown in the Le Pen and Mélenchon cases, and the AFT will not answer it, but its view of what the ECB has told the Trésor is worth having.

Non-residents hold 57.5%, the highest share among the G7, about half of them in the euro area. The non-euro half is the rate-sensitive part: the Japanese sold more than €20bn of OATs between June and November 2024 and about €30bn over the year as the yen carry trade lost its logic ext; the same group is now the swing buyer when OATs yield more than BTPs. The OAT–BTP 10-year spread inverted in August 2026 (OATs 5 bp over BTPs at one point, France the highest-yielding sovereign in the euro area) ext. For a holder of our size the relevant question is not whether non-residents sell in a shock but whether they are still there to buy the €408–526bn of gross issuance a year that Figure 15 implies for 2028–2032 in the status quo.

French insurers and banks (9.6% and 10.5%) are the domestic backstop, and both have limits. Life insurers took a record €44bn of net inflows in 2025 ext, largely into euro funds that buy OATs; those inflows are a function of the OAT yield versus regulated savings (Livret A) and reverse when policy rates rise or when a wealth-tax or assurance-vie tax proposal appears in a budget, which is a live possibility in either 2027 outcome. Their sovereign book is only about 19% of assets after look-through, so the capacity to add is finite. Banks hold OATs largely at amortised cost for liquidity purposes and are therefore insensitive to mark-to-market but sensitive to the sovereign-bank loop through ratings and repo haircuts. Neither group is the buyer of a 2027 supply surge; that is the point of the €140bn figure: it is mostly on the books of holders who can and do leave.

To ask the AFT on holders

  1. Where does the AFT see the marginal buyer of 2027 OAT supply, and how much of the Eurosystem run-off is being absorbed by domestic banks' liquidity books versus non-residents?
  2. Has the Trésor had any indication of how the ECB would read the TPI conditions for France while the EDP is open and off-path?
  3. Does the AFT track the country split of non-resident holders (it does not publish it), and what happened to the Japanese and Asian central-bank bid during the September 2026 widening?

5The 2027 map: Philippe, Le Pen, Mélenchon

The first round is on 18 April 2027 and the second on 2 May; legislative elections follow in June. Marine Le Pen is eligible: the Paris Court of Appeal on 7 July 2026 upheld the conviction but cut the ineligibility to 45 months with 30 suspended, and the firm part had already run ext. The polling average in September has Bardella and Le Pen at 34% and 33.5% in the first round and Philippe at 17.5% when he runs without Attal ext; Mélenchon has closed on Philippe for second place (Odoxa, May 2026: Philippe 17%, Mélenchon 16%, inside the margin of error), although an October survey still has Philippe ahead in every configuration ext. On 6 October the RN published its counter-budget: €140bn of net savings by 2032 against 2026, a deficit below 3% of GDP in 2030, debt from 121% of GDP in 2027 to 112% in 2032, and a balanced-budget rule by referendum ext. Philippe called it "d'une désinvolture spectaculaire"; no independent costing has been published. The five configurations below are not symmetric probabilities: they are the two Assembly outcomes for each of the two front-runners, plus a Mélenchon presidency as the left-tail case, whether or not he has a majority. Reading the map as a holder

Three things follow. First, the distribution of outcomes for the interest bill is skewed the other way from what the polls suggest: against the status quo, the three Philippe-majority and Le Pen cases save €18–34bn a year by 2035, and Mélenchon costs €76bn. A position sized on the status quo is short a put on a left-wing presidency, and long a call on RN fiscal delivery that the market does not yet credit. Second, the 2027 spread is the near-term risk in every case except a Philippe majority: 175–190 bp is above today's 135 bp, and a hung Assembly under either president means budgets by 49.3 and a spread that does not come back below 125–150 bp. Third, no case is a solvency case on this arithmetic except, eventually, Mélenchon: debt at 166% of GDP in 2035 with interest at 7.8% is past Italy in 2012. The others end at 130–144% with interest at 4.8–5.5%. Every case becomes a solvency question only if the pricing loop runs faster than the fiscal loop, which is a question about the ECB and the marginal buyer, not about French arithmetic. That is why the questions in section 7 are about exits, not about levels.

6Other things that matter in the room

Ratings and the crossover. France is A+ stable at S&P (October 2025) and Fitch (September 2025), AA at DBRS, Aa3 negative at Moody's and AA- negative at Scope ext. Italy is on an upgrade path. A Moody's downgrade to A1 would put France at single-A at three of the four agencies that matter for index weights and repo schedules, and the OAT–BTP inversion of August 2026 says the market has already priced that crossover; some analysts had the OAT–Bund spread at 105 bp by end-Q1 2027 on it ext, a level it passed in October 2026. Worth asking the AFT what feedback it has had from index providers and from the ECB collateral desk on the single-A question.

The EDP and the Commission. The procedure opened in July 2024; the January 2025 Council recommendation caps net expenditure growth at 0.8% (2025) and 1.2% (2026–2028) with correction by 2029, and the Commission's spring 2026 forecast (deficit 5.1% in 2026, debt 118.1% at end-2026) has France off the path ext. No scenario in section 5 corrects by 2029. The interaction with the TPI conditions is the reason this is more than a compliance question.

The PLF 2027. The 2026 budget passed on 2 February 2026 by 49.3 after two censure motions failed, at a 5.0% deficit (from 4.6% planned) with concessions to the PS and €6.5bn more for defence ext. The PLF 2027 is presented within weeks and is the last budget before the election: the AFT's 2027 financing programme (published in December) will be built on it. The AFT's Chief Economist will know the assumed 2027 deficit and the debt-service line; they are the numbers to get.

Data the AFT could give and does not. Redbox's French chains are blocked at step A because the État's own cash interest and financing tables (PLF programme 117, the tableau de financement) are not machine-readable to us (OQ-8), and the register is gross of buybacks because rachats are published only in aggregate. Per-line buyback volumes and the programme-117 tables would close the French interest chain to the euro. A request in the room costs nothing.

The CADES and the social perimeter. Eurostat's S.1311 securities run €43bn above the AFT's État total at end-2025 (Figure 1): CADES and the other central bodies. CADES' amortisation schedule (to 2033) is a hidden source of negotiable-debt demand replacement in the late 2020s, and a Le Pen pension rollback would reopen the question of who funds the social deficit.

7Questions to put to Nicolas Maserak

The counterpart is the AFT's Chief Economist and Head of Research, not its chief executive or its head of trading. That changes the questions in three ways. He owns the macro and rates assumptions behind the financing programme, the debt-service projection that feeds the PLF, the investor research and the monthly bulletin; he does not decide the programme, the tenor mix or the buybacks, and he will not comment on politics, the ECB or the rating agencies. So every question below is phrased as "how do you model", "what does your research show", "what is your reading", which he can answer in full. Second, a head of research will engage with the register: lead with the fact that Redbox reconciles security-by-security interest to S.1311 D.41 within ±€6bn and that the fixed lines sit 1–4% above the AFT's totals because buybacks are published only in aggregate, and the data asks follow naturally as an exchange rather than a request. Third, his career before the AFT ran through the Trésor's state-shareholding agency (the APE, managing the State's stakes in EDF, RTE and Enedis) ext: a corporate-finance lens. Frame the interest bill as a refinancing wall and the spread as a cost of capital and he will meet the argument on that ground. Check the spelling and title on aft.gouv.fr before the meeting; the profile could not be verified online.

In the order to raise them

  1. Projection method. What 10-year OAT, spread and BTF rate sit behind the 2027 and 2028 charge de la dette in the PLF 2027, and is the spread held at the forward or given a path? Our register roll gives €89bn and €105bn of general-government interest in the status quo. Do you project line by line, as we do, or on the average cost of the stock?
  2. Cross-check offer. Our security-level interest meets D.41 within ±€6bn since 2012. Where would you expect the residual to sit: the accrual on linkers, the buybacks, or the value-date items? This is the moment to ask for rachats by line and the programme-117 tables in a machine-readable form.
  3. The refinancing wall. The register shows €203bn of OATs maturing in 2027, €246bn in 2028 and €281bn in 2029, the highest share of GDP on record. How much of that has already been bought back, and how much of 2027 do you expect to pre-fund in Q4 2026?
  4. Demand research. What does your investor research show about the marginal buyer since the Eurosystem run-off reached about €10bn a month? Which holder group absorbed the September widening? Then: the country split of non-residents that the AFT does not publish, and whether the Japanese and Asian official bid returned once OATs cheapened past BTPs.
  5. Maturity at issue. The average maturity of fixed-rate issuance fell from 12.6 years (2021) to 10.3 (2026 to date). Is that a demand fact or a cost choice in your research, and at what spread differential does the 20- to 30-year sector reopen?
  6. Bills as buffer. In 2008–2009 the BTF stock doubled to €214bn. Is that still the research view of the shock absorber, or is there an internal ceiling on the bill share of the stock?
  7. Spread feedback. The Cour des comptes has urged a spread-feedback assumption in debt-service projections. Has your team modelled one, and what elasticity of the spread to the debt ratio do you find? We declare spread paths outright (150–250 bp in 2027) rather than a mechanical feedback, because the pricing channel moves faster than the debt ratio.
  8. Cost-at-risk. What does your cost-at-risk model say a 100 bp parallel shift adds to the 2028 charge, and how much of that is the bill stock? Our register gives about €2bn a year with no lag from the bills alone.
  9. Ratings and index thresholds, as research. What have you seen in investor mandates at the single-A boundary, and did any index or collateral treatment change after the S&P and Fitch moves?
  10. TPI, as a fact question. Which of the TPI's four conditions does the Trésor consider France meets today? He will not comment on ECB intentions; he can answer a question about the fiscal framework.
  11. Linkers and green OATs. With 11% of the stock indexed and uplift accruing at about €6bn a year, is the linker programme being scaled to the demand you see, and does the green OAT still price through the curve?
  12. The election, without naming candidates. How does the AFT plan the Q2 2027 issuance calendar around the two rounds, and what did the June 2024 dissolution teach the desk about syndication timing?
Two questions from the working draft were dropped on purpose: whether the AFT models politics, and whether it would lengthen maturity if a government asked. Both put a research head in a position he cannot take on the record. Everything above stays inside what he can say.

Caveats

  • The register is gross of buybacks on the fixed lines (1–4% above the AFT); redemptions and gross issuance in the engine are correspondingly high. Net issuance and interest are barely affected.
  • Interest is on the accrual basis and general government; the État's budgetary "charge de la dette" (about €58bn in 2026 ext) is a narrower, cash concept and will always be lower.
  • Inflation and the Bund curve are common to all scenarios; growth differs but enters only through nominal GDP, because the primary balance is declared as an outturn in euros. The engine is an arithmetic of the register, not a macro model: the growth and spread assumptions are not derived from the fiscal paths, and a recession or an ECB easing cycle would move every line.
  • The issuance mix and the bill stock are held fixed in every scenario for comparability; a debt office facing 250–300 bp would shorten, which lowers interest early and raises refinancing risk later.
  • The strict file's 2025 interest is an AMECO-stitched value (grade B); the Eurostat outturn arrives in October 2026.
  • Holder shares and every political and market fact marked ext are unverified against primary sources; the sources are listed below.
